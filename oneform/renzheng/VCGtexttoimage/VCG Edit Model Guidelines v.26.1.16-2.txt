Edit Instruction Following measures whether the model attempted and applied the change requested in the prompt.
This dimension focuses on whether the requested edit was carried out, not on how well it was executed, how realistic it looks, or whether the output
is visually polished.
You should evaluate only whether the model followed the instruction at a functional level—
Did it make the requested change, or did it fail to do so?
Detailed quality issues will be evaluated under other dimensions.
Ask yourself the following questions when evaluating this dimension:

Safety Flag
Grading Dimensions
Leaving Comments

1

Did the model attempt the required modification?

2

Did it add, remove, or change the correct object/attribute?

3

Did the output demonstrate the intent of the instruction?

4

Did the model misunderstand the prompt entirely?

Leaving Comments

Structural Integrity refers to the anatomical correctness and visual integrity of subjects and objects in a generated image. Evaluators
determine whether each element is formed correctly and consistently, based on the prompt—considering both explicitly stated and implied
anatomical or structural details.
• Structural Integrity should be judged without factoring in any other dimensions.
• Correct Parts and Proportions: Unless otherwise specified by the prompt, subjects should have the typical number, proportions, and
arrangement of their constituent parts.
◦ Humans: The front view of a human head should typically show two eyes, a nose, a mouth, and two ears. If the body is visible, it usually has
two arms with hands and two legs with feet. Pay particular attention to the eyes to ensure there are no issues with gaze or placement.
◦ Animals: For instance, a tiger would normally have four legs, a tail (depending on the viewpoint), sharp teeth, and claws.
◦ Objects: Buildings usually display roofs, windows, doors, etc. Cars have four wheels, windows, and doors. Airplanes have two wings, engines,
etc.
◦ Text: Any text appearing in the image should be clearly legible and free of errors. Text should read as meaningful language rather than
gibberish or random characters, and it must not contain spelling or grammatical mistakes.
• Prompt-Relative Evaluation: Structural integrity is judged based on what the prompt requests or implies, not on a general standard of "realworld" accuracy. A three-headed dog is structurally sound if the prompt asks for a three-headed dog.
◦ Explicit vs. Implied Features:
▪ Explicit: Features directly mentioned in the prompt (e.g., "a blue car with five wheels").
▪ Implied: Features not directly mentioned, but reasonably expected based on common understanding (e.g., "a man" implies a human form
with two arms, two legs, etc.).

• Internal Consistency: Even if the prompt describes something fantastical, the generated object should be internally consistent. A three-headed

dog should have three well-formed heads, not heads merging into each other or floating in space.
• Distortions or Artifacts:
◦ A distortion or an artifact can directly affect Structural Integrity. Penalise any distortions or artifacts that compromise the realism or accuracy

of the depiction. If the style is intentionally stylized, ensure structural features still look coherent with that style.

▪ Distortions: A body part or object appears twisted, pulled out of shape, or malformed (e.g., “melting” limbs).
▪ Artifacts: Unnatural or unintended objects that do not logically fit the scene.
• Highly Stylized Images:
◦ When evaluating highly stylized images (e.g., cartoons, anime, illustrations, abstract art, surrealism), structural integrity must be interpreted

within the context of the chosen style.
▪ Stylized art intentionally departs from real-world proportions, physics, anatomy, or realism.
• Do not penalize intentional exaggeration or unrealistic features that are normal for the style (e.g., oversized eyes in anime,

simplified anatomy in cartoons, exaggerated shapes, non-realistic color schemes).
Leaving Comments

• Only mark structural integrity issues when the error breaks the internal logic of the style, for example, distorted shapes, missing

parts, or inconsistent anatomy relative to the style.
• If the style supports creative or non-literal interpretations, the image should be evaluated by its stylistic coherence, not realism.
• Evaluate structural integrity relative to the intended style, not real-world realism. Penalize only when the structure is broken for the

style, not just unrealistic.

Preservation of Unedited Areas
This dimension evaluates whether the parts of the image that were not supposed to be edited remain visually consistent with the original image.
Edits should only affect the areas specified in the instruction.
All other regions of the image like background details, objects, people, lighting, color, texture, and composition, should remain unchanged unless

This dimension helps ensure that the model does not accidentally introduce new changes, distortions, or artifacts outside the requested edit

Visual quality refers to how well an image is rendered and presented. It ensures that the image appears clear, coherent, and visually balanced, so
viewers can easily perceive the intended subject and details without distraction from technical flaws such as poor contrast, blurriness, or
rendering issues. Visual quality evaluates how the image looks, not what it depicts. This criteria also evaluates how well the edited content
integrates into the original image and how visually coherent, natural, and artifact-free the final image appears.
While Edit Instruction Following assesses whether the requested change is applied, Visual Quality measures how well that change is rendered
visually. A visually high-quality edit should look natural, coherent, and well-blended into the surrounding scene. The focus of this section is purely
on visual integration, rendering quality, and aesthetic coherence.

ℹ

When an edit modifies either the foreground or the background, you should check that both areas remain visually consistent. Changes to lighting, shadows,
perspective, or environmental conditions in one part of the image should be reflected appropriately in the other. Inconsistencies (e.g., a bright subject in a dark
environment) should be flagged in visual quality.
