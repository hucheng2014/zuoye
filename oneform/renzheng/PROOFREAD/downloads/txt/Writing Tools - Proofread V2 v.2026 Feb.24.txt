Introduction
The goal of the Proofreading feature is to correct all grammatical errors in the user's input text while preserving the original tone, style, and
register as much as possible.
Your evaluation of the response/output will focus on two dimensions: Correctness and Completeness.
• Correctness verifies whether all edits in a response are correct and adhere to the Minimal Edit Principle.
• Completeness verifies whether all errors in the input text are detected and corrected properly.

Guideline Learning Path
There are many nuances to Proofread and understandably so, given that the feature has to meet user expectations in different cultural,
linguistic, formal and informal contexts.
To navigate the complexity, begin the guideline review by getting familiar with:
1. Minimal Edit Principle
2. Principles for Handling Ambiguous or Informal Contexts
These principles will help you prioritize and navigate complex scenarios.

02 Proofreading Grading Principles
I. What is the Minimal Edit Principle?
Proofreading edits must be strictly minimal—addressing only grammar, punctuation, capitalization, spelling, or formatting issues as lightly as
possible.
No changes in the response should alter the meaning, style, tone, register, or intent of the input text.
Note: register refers to the level of formality or style of speaking or writing used for a particular purpose or situation.
If any one of the following is violated, you will penalize the response under Correctness dimensions.
1. Preserve semantic content
2. Preserve tone, register, style, and formality
3. Preserve pronoun referentiality
4. Preserve proper noun referentiality
5. Preserve expressivity, colloquialisms or slang (this section needs your particular attention – PLEASE REVIEW)
6. Preserve formatting
7. Preserve Local Punctuations and Formatting
In the next few pages, you will see the examples of these rules.

!! While most of the rules and the examples are straightforward, we do want to call out the rule to Preserve expressivity, colloquialisms or
slang. Make sure to review that section.

I. Minimal Edit Principle - Rules and Examples
1. Preserve Semantic Content
Does the response maintain the original meaning and intent of the input text without introducing new content or altering expression?
Vocabulary, emphasis, and degree must be preserved exactly — even small lexical substitutions (including synonyms) or simplifications
count as unnecessary edits (because they cause undesirable semantic shifts).

Violations (bad corrections) include:
•

"help fix the issue" → "resolve the issue" (when "help fix" is grammatically correct)

•

"somewhat successful" → "successful" (removing hedge words changes meaning)

•

"most important" → "important" (removing qualifier changes emphasis)

•

"kindest regards" → "kind regards" (changing degree of politeness)

•

"saddened to hear" → "sad to hear" (rewording without grammatical need)

•

"take part in" → "participate in" (substituting with synonym unnecessarily)

2. Preserve Tone, Register, Style, and Formality (Rules)
Does the response maintain the same level of formality, politeness, and tone as the input text, respecting all relevant linguistic and cultural
markers in the language? This includes preserving:
•

Intended honorifics, verb forms, and polite vs. impolite pronouns

•

Formal vs. informal vocabulary and set phrases

•

Modal verbs, hedging, and indirect expressions

•

Sentence structure and polite interjections

•

Forms of address, titles, and honorifics

Minor adjustments that are strictly required to fix grammatical errors are allowed and do not count as violations, even if they slightly change
the surface form.
However, avoid upgrading or downgrading the overall level of formality unless it is required to correct an actual error.

2. Preserve colloquial or informal style.

• If the text is clearly informal: Do not formalize or standardize text that is clearly informal. Avoid changing colloquial expressions or

• Ex: “gonna” → do not change to “going to”

• If the text's formality is ambiguous: It is acceptable to make edits that increase formality if they also improve grammatical correctness.
• In these ambiguous cases, changing “gonna” to “going to” would be considered a necessary and correct edit.
• When in doubt, err on the side of accepting such changes if they enhance grammaticality and/or clarity.

3. Accept reasonable interpretations of unclear text.
When the meaning of the input text is truly unclear, there are two acceptable outcomes for a response:

• A Reasonable Edit is Made: If the response makes a plausible, good-faith edit that clarifies the text, this is acceptable. The
interpretation should be logical and shouldn't invent a meaning that seems unlikely.

• No Edit is Made: It is also acceptable for the response to leave the ambiguous text as-is. Do not penalize the lack of edits—since
it s reasonable to avoid changes when the intended meaning is genuinely ambiguous.

Step 1: Review the Original Input Text
In this first step, review the original input text with these questions in mind, as you will use the information gathered in this step to answer the first
question.

• Is there any grammatical error in the input text?
• Is part of the input text ambiguous due to (lack of) punctuation?
• ex: "I like cooking my family and my pets." is ambiguous. It should be "I like cooking, my family, and my pets."
• Is the input text too vague and completely incomprehensible?
What counts as a grammatical error?
A grammatical error is an objective violation of the foundational rules of language, such as incorrect spelling, punctuation, subject-verb agreement, or
sentence structure.
This definition excludes subjective assessments of writing, meaning issues of tone, style, or unnatural phrasing are not considered grammatical errors.

Japanese Guidelines (ja_JP)
There are some complex areas in the Japanese language:
•

Script: The Japanese writing system uses a combination of three scripts: Hiragana, Katakana, and Kanji. While some flexibility exists, many words
and phrases have a standard script they are most commonly and appropriately written in.

•

Tone: Tone in Japanese is crucial for conveying the appropriate level of politeness (e.g., casual, polite, and formal/honorific). This is not just about
word choice; it is built into the grammar and directly affects how verbs and adjectives are conjugated.

•

Conjugation: Japanese verbs and adjectives change their endings (conjugate) to express tense (past/present), politeness, and mood (e.g.,
conditional, imperative).

For MSA and Arabic dialects, the Minimal Edit Principle applies. The response should preserve the tone, style, and register of the input text.

• If an input text is written in an Arabic dialect or a mixture of MSA and a dialect, the output should preserve the original register and corrects
grammatical errors only.

• Change in register or formality is considered a violation of the Minimal Edit Principle and should be penalized.
This task requires your attention to the authenticity of the response in the respective dialect. To learn more about it, refer to the Authenticity in Syntax
and Morphology table at the end of this section [1].

⚠ Guideline Callouts
Given that Arabic communities use both MSA and dialects, we'd like to call out some grading rules to align your expectations with the engineering team.
1. When an input is written in an Arabic dialect, the response (output) should reflect the common, standardized practices of native speakers of that
dialect.
2. The response should always preserve dialect variations in the input text, including phonetic variations.If the input text contains the phonetic variations
in dialect, the response should not modify them. This is considered unnecessary modifications.
When a response does not meet any of these requirements, it should be penalized.

Hinglish Guidelines (hi_LATN)
Hinglish is a blend of Romanised Hindi and English. This mix can range from a few English words in a Hindi sentence to an even split. Your
corrections should result in a Hinglish output that mirrors the tone and level of code-switching present in the input.
For Hinglish, the Minimal Edit Principle applies. The response should preserve the tone, style, and register of the input text. The goal is to
refine Hinglish text by correcting clear errors while respecting its nature as a mixed language.

⚠ Note
Given that Hinglish includes both Indian English (en_IN) and Hindi Devanagari, below are the expectations for outputs / responses generated by the model:

• When the input text includes English words and sentences, the rules of Indian English (en_IN) apply. This includes correcting clear spelling errors.
• When the input text includes Hindi Latin words and sentences, the grammatical rules of Hindi Devanagari apply, simply transliterated into the Latin
script.

• Word Variations: Multiple accepted spellings for a Hindi Latin word are permissible as long as they are phonetically correct and not overly shortened
chat-speak. A good response should correct clear errors, not to enforce a single stylistic choice among several valid ones.

• Important: There should be no Devanagari present in a response, unless the input text has any. If this occurs, please penalize the response.
• Skip the task: If the input text is largely in Devanagari Hindi (ex: आज का वातावरण ब त राब kal accha tha.), please click on Skip the Current Task and
select "The language or content in the input text is not typical of this locale.”

response would use “karte .”

•

Incorrect use of gender should be corrected: In Hindi, inanimate objects have a grammatical gender. Errors in verb conjugation related
to object gender should be corrected to ensure grammatical accuracy.
