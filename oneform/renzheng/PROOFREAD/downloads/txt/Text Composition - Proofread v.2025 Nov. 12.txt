The goal of the Proofreading feature is to correct grammatical errors in the user's input text while preserving the original
wording and style as much as possible. If no grammatical error is found, a repeating of the input text is deemed as the only
correct action.
Unnecessary change in wording, style, or tone would impact the grading of Following Instructions, Groundedness, and
Satisfaction.
This guideline is an addendum to the response evaluation in the Preference Ranking Guidelines. It highlights crucial guidelines
in Following-Instructions, Concision and Truthfulness. Rules pertaining to Comprehensiveness and Style, Tone, & Grammar are
overridden, branching out to create two new dimensions. It is tailored specifically for proofreading tasks.

Following Instructions
Overview
Unnecessary Modifications and Different Locales
Due to subtle variations in tone, style, and expression within a language, what counts as "unnecessary modification" vs "grammatical fix" can
slightly change in different locales. Some modifications count as grammatical fix, while some don't.
In the grading scale and examples section for Following Instructions , we provided examples for ja_JP, zh_CN, and Arabic languages showcase
such subtlety and calibrate grading.
However, we'd like to call out some guiding principles here:
Generally speaking, the response should preserve the intent, wording, style, and tone as much as possible.

• This means that even when the input text contains slightly unnatural phrasing, the change made by the response to improve fluidity would still

be considered unnecessary modifications.
• Notes on rewording and tone change:
• Rewording: Rewording is allowed only if the original word or phrase does not make sense, is obviously incorrect, or if it is misaligned with
the context of the input text.
• Tone change: In languages like Japanese, Korean, or some others that use honorific speech to show respect or formality, most of the tone
change or refinement in the response would be considered as unnecessary modifications--for the scope of this task. A tone change would
only be allowed when the input text shows obvious disagreement between the tone and the verb or adjective conjugations.
• Registers: In many language communities, a variety of languages (registers) are used. For example, in Arabic communities, Modern
Standard Arabic (MSA) is used alongside other varieties of Arabic. If the input text is in an Arabic dialect or uses a mixture of MSA and
dialect, the output should preserve the same style and register.
• The Instruction Following dimension requires graders to be more strict in order to verify whether a response is aligned with the feature goal
(stated in the Introduction).
• Note that for responses that make unnecessary changes in wording, style, and tone, they should also be penalized under the Satisfaction
dimension (Slightly Unsatisfying). Review the examples in the ja_JP and zh_CN sections for more details.

The truthfulness in text composition is basically the groundedness of the output text to the input.

Groundedness

Composition

In the text composition evaluation tasks, the speaker provides the input text. The assistant s response must be truthful or grounded to the
contextual text without contradicting information. The factual accuracy of the response is not in the scope of the whole proofreading evaluation.
Any irrelevant crucial information added in the output is considered less grounded. Alternative word choices, sentence structures or writing
styles, which convey the same meaning, are out of this dimension.

Localization

Locale-Specific Guidelines

Localization is the process of adapting a product, service, or content to a specific target market. A well-localized response will give the impression
the assistant is designed specifically for the target locale. To achieve this, responses should:
• Be written with native-level fluency, unless otherwise requested
• Things such as spelling, vocabulary, and grammar should all be correct
• Consider the context of the locale when providing information
• It should provide the most relevant information for the target locale when possible
• However, it should not be unnecessarily restricted answers about the locale (see "overly-localized content" below)
• Use tone, phrases, and idioms that feel natural to a user in that locale

Japanese Guidelines

• It should not overuse words, phrases, or idioms to the point of sounding like a stereotype

Chinese Guidelines

• Use correct formatting for dates, addresses, etc, and correct units of measurement for distance, temperature, currency, etc.

Arabic Languages
Guidelines

• Not draw attention to the user's locale in a way that makes it seem outside the norm (see "over-specification" below)
• The purpose of this section is to identify anything about the response that is improper for the target locale.

Specialized Guidelines for Japanese

• Change in scripts: The Japanese writing system uses a combination of three scripts: Hiragana, Katakana, and Kanji. Some words and phrases

are commonly written in Kanji, while some are in Hiragana or Katakana.
- If an input text contains words or phrases that do not get written in their usual script, and the response corrects it, this correction would
count as a grammatical fix (Fully following).
- On the contrary, if the scripts used in input text are correct, and the response modifies how a certain word or phrase is written, that would
count as an unnecessary modification (Partially following).
• Change in tone: as mentioned in the guiding principles above, most of the tone change or refinement in the response would be considered as
unnecessary modifications--for the scope of this task.
- A tone change would only be allowed when the input text shows an obvious disagreement between the tone and the verb or adjective
conjugations.
• Change in conjugations (for verbs or adjectives):
- If the input text has obvious conjugation errors (ex: a tense error), the correction made by the response would be considered as Fully
following.
- If the input text contains a verb form that sounds slightly unnatural due to its conjugation but is still grammatically correct, then the change
made by the response is not allowed. It would be considered as Partially following.

For Hinglish, the general rule of preserving style and tone applies. The goal is to refine Hinglish text by correcting clear errors while respecting its
nature as a mixed language.

Comprehensiveness
Composition
Localization

Hinglish is a blend of Romanised Hindi and English. This mix can range from a few English words in a Hindi sentence to an even split. Your
corrections should result in a Hinglish output that mirrors the tone and level of code-switching present in the input.

Harmfulness
Satisfaction

Expectations for outputs / responses

Locale-Specific Guidelines

Given that Hinglish includes both Indian English (en_IN) and Hindi Devanagari, below are the expectations for outputs / responses generated
by the model:

Japanese Guidelines
Chinese Guidelines

Guidelines

•

When the input text includes English words and sentences, the rules of Indian English (en_IN) apply. This includes correcting clear
spelling errors.

•

When the input text includes Hindi Latin words and sentences, the grammatical rules of Hindi Devanagari apply, simply transliterated
into the Latin script.

•

Word Variations: Multiple accepted spellings for a Hindi Latin word are permissible as long as they are phonetically correct and not
overly shortened chat-speak. A good response should correct clear errors, not to enforce a single stylistic choice among several valid
ones.

•

response under Composition, Localization, and Satisfaction.

Chinese Guidelines

The response contains several errors.
▪
"Ahí" should be "Hay" in this context: In Spanish, "hay" and "ahí" are often confused. "Hay" is an impersonal verb form of "haber" meaning

should have been replaced with "hay".
▪
"Guarerria" incorrectly fixed: "guarerria" should have been fixed to "guarrería", and not "guareña" nor "guarrada."
▪
Unlocalized punctuation in greeting: The correct format of a greeting in es_ES uses a comma between the greeting and the subject, and a colon
in the end. It should be "Hola, colegas:”
▪

Hola, colegas:
Soy yo otra vez. Otra vez con mis historias. Está lloviendo, hay barro y está todo hecho una guarrería. Intento mantener la mente positiva,
