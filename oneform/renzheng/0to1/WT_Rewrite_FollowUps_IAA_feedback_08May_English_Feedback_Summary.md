# WT Rewrite Follow-Ups IAA Feedback — English Summary

> Source: `WT_Rewrite FollowUps_IAA feedback_08May.pdf`  
> Topic: IAA-based calibration for the Rewrite Follow-Ups workflow.  
> Purpose: Highlight the rubric areas where analysts disagree most often and clarify how to apply the standards consistently.

---

## 1. Context

This document is not a full replacement for the Rewrite Follow-Ups guideline. It is a calibration note based on Inter-Analyst Agreement (IAA). The issues are ordered from the lowest agreement / most recurring disagreement areas.

The main weak spots are:

- Step 2: Personalization
- Step 3: Grammar & Clarity and Tone Appropriateness
- Step 4: Follow-up Suggestions
- Step 5: Suggestion Adherence, Meaning Preservation, and Quality Improvement
- Localization

A general rule: apply the current Writing Tools rubric, not personal preference, another project’s rules, or a generic language standard.

---

## 2. Personalization — Step 2

This is the highest-risk dimension across locales, often below 40% agreement.

Key points:

- The sample outputs are not mapped to the actual user as factual examples. They only illustrate a writing-style pattern.
- Focus on style signals: sentence length, vocabulary complexity, formality markers, and expression habits.
- Do not penalize factual differences between the sample outputs and the rewrite.
- “Well matched” does not mean the rewrite is identical to the samples. It means the rewrite consistently reflects the same style category.
- One formal word in an otherwise casual rewrite should not automatically lower the rating to “Partially matched.”
- For Asian and European locales, labels such as “formal,” “casual,” or “advanced vocabulary” are culturally loaded. Anchor your judgment to the task’s sample outputs, not to your own default cultural baseline.

Bottom line: **Personalization is about style matching, not factual matching.**

---

## 3. Fix Errors — Step 3 Grammar & Clarity

This dimension looks simple, but analysts often mix up the questions.

Separate the three checks:

1. **Q1:** Were errors already present in the original fixed?
2. **Q2:** Did the rewrite introduce new errors?
3. **Q3:** Is the rewrite generally clear and well written?

Common mistakes:

- If the original has no clear grammar error, mark **Not applicable**, not Yes.
- Q1 is only about whether old errors were fixed.
- Q2 is only about new errors introduced by the rewrite.
- If an original error was not fixed, that is a Q1 failure, not a Q2 failure.
- In languages where “error” can be ambiguous, such as Chinese punctuation/word choice or European gender agreement, first decide whether there is a clear error at all.

Bottom line: **Old errors, new errors, and overall clarity must be evaluated separately.**

---

## 4. Preserve Style — Step 5 Meaning Preservation Q2

This question asks whether the second rewrite preserved style elements from the first rewrite that were unrelated to the selected suggestion.

How to evaluate:

1. Mentally isolate what the selected suggestion targets, such as `Soften tone` or `Make it shorter`.
2. Then check everything outside that target: vocabulary, sentence structure, formality, length, and overall style pattern.
3. Only changes in those unrelated areas should count as failures.

Important distinctions:

- Natural side effects of applying the suggestion are acceptable. For example, softening tone may slightly change sentence structure.
- If the suggestion is only about brevity but the rewrite changes from formal vocabulary to casual vocabulary, that is an unrelated style drift.
- Do not conflate tone and style. Tone is emotional register and confidence level; style is structure and vocabulary pattern.

Bottom line: **Only penalize style drift that is unrelated to the selected suggestion.**

---

## 5. Apply Suggestion — Step 5 Suggestion Adherence Q1

The standard is whether the selected suggestion was visibly implemented with noticeable changes directly related to it.

Practical checks:

- Compare the first and second rewrites side by side.
- If the effect of the suggestion is immediately visible, mark Yes.
- Broad suggestions such as `Keep it natural` or `Preserve informal tone` do not require a complete transformation.
- For broad suggestions, look for 2–3 concrete markers: word choices, sentence starters, contractions, punctuation, or tone markers.
- For tone/register suggestions, judge according to the locale’s communication norms rather than English-language assumptions.

Bottom line: **The selected suggestion must have a visible effect; broad suggestions can be proven through concrete markers.**

---

## 6. Negative Side Effects — Step 5 Quality Improvement Q2

This question should fail only when applying the suggestion introduced a clear, concrete new problem.

Do not over-penalize:

- Do not mark a negative side effect just because you personally prefer another phrasing.
- Do not treat expected length increase as a problem when the suggestion is `Add more detail`.
- Minor rewriting or a small word-count change, such as 20 words to 22 words, is not automatically an issue.

Legitimate failures include:

- A meaningful nuance was lost, such as a qualifier, hedge, caveat, or important distinction.
- The text now has a grammar, meaning, tone, or factual problem that was not present before.
- The length becomes genuinely excessive and harms usability.

Bottom line: **A negative side effect must be a real new problem, not a preference.**

---

## 7. Redundancy — Step 4 Follow-up Suggestions

Redundancy means two or more suggestions are nearly identical or would lead to essentially the same second rewrite.

How to test it:

- Do not judge only by surface wording.
- Imagine the result of choosing each suggestion.
- If two suggestions would produce meaningfully the same rewrite, they are redundant.
- Example: `Keep it brief` and `Cut unnecessary words` are redundant.
- Example: `Keep it brief` and `Soften the tone` are not redundant because they target different dimensions.

Bottom line: **Judge redundancy by the rewrite result the suggestions would produce.**

---

## 8. Tone Balance — Step 3 Tone Appropriateness Q2

This dimension is weak in several European locales, especially French, German, Spanish, and Italian.

The question is not simply whether the rewrite sounds natural. It asks whether the rewrite balances:

- the user’s demonstrated writing style, and
- the situational requirements of the current context.

Important notes:

- Professional communication norms differ by locale.
- French professional writing may be more formal; German may be more direct; Spanish may be warmer.
- Use the sample outputs as the primary reference for the user’s baseline style.
- If the rewrite drifts away from the user’s style without a contextual reason, it should fail.
- If the drift is justified by the context, such as writing to a supervisor or making a formal request, it can pass.

Bottom line: **Tone Balance means balancing user personality with situational appropriateness.**

---

## 9. Localization

Writing Tools has its own localization rules.

- Do not use LE guidelines as the reference point.
- Do not report spelling or grammar issues under Localization.
- Localization should focus on language, region, culture, format, and local expression norms.

---

## 10. Quick Checklist

1. Personalization: evaluate style, not factual alignment with samples.
2. Grammar Q1: old errors fixed? If no clear old error, use N/A.
3. Grammar Q2: new errors introduced? Do not use it for unfixed old errors.
4. Preserve Style: only penalize unrelated style drift.
5. Apply Suggestion: look for visible direct effects; broad suggestions need concrete markers.
6. Negative Side Effects: mark only real new problems, not personal preferences.
7. Redundancy: test whether suggestions would create substantially different rewrites.
8. Tone Balance: calibrate to the locale and the user’s sample style.
9. Localization: use Writing Tools rules, not LE rules or grammar/spelling criteria.
